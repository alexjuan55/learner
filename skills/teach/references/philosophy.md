# Why one-to-one, and why connection is the goal

Two sources, two halves of the same argument. This file is background — read it
once. `SKILL.md` carries everything you need per session.

## Half one: one teacher, one mind

Source: Eero Alvar, *How I Use AI to Learn Things* — https://youtu.be/kzcI5F4tGiU

Usual learning is many-to-many, and both directions waste effort.

**One outlet teaches many.** A book, course, or video made for a crowd cannot be
optimal for one mind. Optimal teaching depends entirely on the learner's current
understanding: the whole arc from here to the goal, and each explanation on the
way. The path should sit at the **edge of understanding**:

- do not reteach what they already hold
- do not present what they cannot yet take in

Ideally: one teacher, one student.

**One student learns from many outlets.** Switching styles, notations,
reliability, and interfaces costs mental effort that isn't spent on the material.
The deeper cost is **trust**: an unfamiliar outlet makes the brain hedge. It will
not fully commit a fact until the source feels proven. Two identical explanations
of the hairy ball theorem — one from a trusted teacher, one from a stranger — are
not learned the same way.

Ideally: one student, one teacher.

**One interface, many sources.** "Only one teacher means only one perspective"
confuses *source* with *interface*. The teacher aggregates many sources and
delivers them through one interface. Perspectives are not lost.

With AI, trust is not built over years. It is **engineered**: verification and
fact-checking exist so the information is correct *and* so the learner can stop
hedging.

Two principles follow: **optimized teaching** (fitted to this mind, at the edge),
and **optimized allocation of mental resources** (concentrate struggle in the
material; the system absorbs planning, sourcing, verifying, ordering). Do not
remove difficulty. Maximize struggle *in the thing being learned*.

## Half two: connection is what understanding *is*

Two brains can hold the same propositions and look identical from outside — same
answers to the same questions. One holds a pile of **disconnected lone facts**
(A). The other holds a few **core truths** from which every one of those facts is
derivable (B), so to it the facts are obviously connected.

That connection *is* understanding. It isn't a nice side effect of knowing the
facts; it is the difference between holding them and not.

- Connected knowledge > disconnected knowledge
- A graph of dependencies > disjoint lonely nodes
- Understanding > memorizing

Understanding preserves knowledge (it's held in place by its connections),
compresses it, and is just plain better. The two principles in `SKILL.md` exist to
build that graph: **Principle i** adds nodes he can safely commit to, **Principle
ii** adds the edges between them.

### The click

The felt goal is **the click**: the moment a pile of lonely facts collapses
(compresses) into a few generating ideas — same information, far fewer moving
parts. When teaching lands, that collapse is what it feels like from the inside.
Aim for it. If a lesson ends with him holding more separate things than he
started with, the lesson failed even if every fact was correct.

### Why the brain hedges (the mechanism both principles exploit)

**The brain won't fully commit to a fact it isn't sure is safe to lock in.** If
something more fundamental might later contradict it, committing is risky — it
would force an expensive update. So the brain hedges, and the fact never really
lands. It can recite it and still not hold it.

- **Principle i** removes the risk by starting from facts nothing can contradict.
- **Principle ii** removes it by making the fact feel derived rather than
  decreed — if he could have discovered it, it isn't arbitrary, so there's
  nothing to hedge against.

Both are, mechanically, the same move: make commitment safe. That is why they are
not tips but the whole method. Any technique that makes commitment feel riskier —
asserting something he must take on faith, skipping the motivation, teaching above
his edge — works against the grain and won't stick no matter how well phrased.

## Corollary: what "accurate" is for

One confidently-delivered hallucination poisons trust, and trust is the thing the
entire single-interface argument rests on. A wrong unconditional truth doesn't
just mislead — it corrupts every node built on top of it, and those nodes now feel
*derived*, so the error is harder to dislodge than a plain unknown. Verify before
you teach as fact; say plainly when a check changed what you were about to say.
